 1776: The “misguided Americans” and their “inadmissible claim of Independence”

 On this day in 1776, the[Declaration of Independence](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Declaration-of-Independence)was approved by the [Continental Congress](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Continental-Congress). The document—which announced the separation of the [13 North American British colonies](https://www.britannica.com/topic/American-colonies)from Great Britain—was met with scorn by British officials. Fighting dragged on for years, and when the [American Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/American-Revolution) finally ended in 1783, the colonies were victorious and went on to form the [United States of America](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States). July 4 subsequently became a national holiday.

The death of Jefferson and Adams

 Two major figures of the [American Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/American-Revolution) who became U.S. presidents, [Thomas Jefferson](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Thomas-Jefferson) and [John Adams](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Adams-president-of-United-States), died on this day in 1826—50 years to the day after the adoption of the [Declaration of Independence](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Declaration-of-Independence). Adams’s last words were “Jefferson survives.” He was unaware that Jefferson had died hours earlier.

2019

 Pope [Francis](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Francis-I-pope) and President [Vladimir Putin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin) of Russia met in Vatican City and discussed, among other issues, [ongoing fighting in Ukraine](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ukraine/The-Poroshenko-administration#ref351575) and the schism between the [Orthodox Church of Ukraine](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Orthodox-Church-of-Ukraine) and the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Russian-Orthodox-Church).

 Scientists at the [Large Hadron Collider](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Large-Hadron-Collider) at [CERN](https://www.britannica.com/topic/CERN) announced that they had detected an interesting signal that was later confirmed to be from a [Higgs boson](https://www.britannica.com/science/Higgs-boson).

 The [Republic of the Philippines](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Philippines/World-War-II#ref360181) was proclaimed, and [Manuel Roxas](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Manuel-Roxas-y-Acuna) became its first president.

1939

 On an appreciation day in his honor, American [baseball](https://www.britannica.com/sports/baseball) player [Lou Gehrig](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lou-Gehrig), who had been forced to retire months earlier due to [ALS](https://www.britannica.com/science/amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis), gave a memorable speech in which he claimed to be “the luckiest man on the face of the earth.” _[[How much do you know about baseball?](https://www.britannica.com/quiz/baseball)]_

1930

 The [Nation of Islam](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nation-of-Islam) is founded by [Wallace Fard](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Wallace-D-Fard) (later Wali Fard Muhammad) in [Detroit](https://www.britannica.com/place/Detroit). A religious movement that combines elements of Muslim teachings with Black nationalism, the Nation of Islam was created with a mission “to teach the downtrodden and defenseless Black people a thorough knowledge of God and of themselves.” _[[Learn more about the people, places, and events that shaped the 1930s](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Timeline-of-the-1930s).]_

![Image 15: Jack Johnson](https://cdn.britannica.com/57/78357-050-1AADB486/Jack-Johnson-1909.jpg?w=260&h=196&c=crop)

 Library of Congress, Washington, D.C.

1910

 In what was billed as the “Fight of the Century,” African American [boxer](https://www.britannica.com/sports/boxing)[Jack Johnson](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jack-Johnson) defeated [James Jackson Jeffries](https://www.britannica.com/biography/James-Jackson-Jeffries), who was considered the “Great White Hope.” Johnson’s victory led to nationwide celebrations by Black Americans that were sometimes met by violence from white people, resulting in more than 20 deaths across the country.

1865

[Lewis Carroll](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lewis-Carroll)’s [Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland](https://www.britannica.com/) was published, though the first print run was soon recalled because of quality issues; a new first edition was released in November. _[[Take our quiz about famous literary characters](https://www.britannica.com/quiz/getting-into-character).]_

![Image 18: “Walt Whitman, an American, one of the roughs, a kosmos”](https://cdn.britannica.com/80/26880-050-CEB5F414/Walt-Whitman-photograph-Mathew-Brady.jpg?w=260&h=196&c=crop)

 Library of Congress, Washington, D.C.

1855

 American author [Walt Whitman](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Walt-Whitman) first published [Leaves of Grass](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Leaves-of-Grass-by-Whitman), a landmark in the history of [American literature](https://www.britannica.com/art/American-literature); undergoing numerous revisions, the [poetry](https://www.britannica.com/art/poetry) collection became known for its unconventional language and its celebration of the human body and sexual passion. _[[Sort fact from fiction in our poetry quiz](https://www.britannica.com/quiz/the-abcs-of-poetry-fact-or-fiction).]_

![Image 19: Henry David Thoreau](https://cdn.britannica.com/42/9042-050-9C83EE9C/Henry-David-Thoreau-portrait-Samuel-Worcester-Rowse-1854.jpg?w=260&h=196&c=crop)

1845

 Essayist and philosopher [Henry David Thoreau](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Henry-David-Thoreau) moved to his retreat at [Walden Pond](https://www.britannica.com/place/Walden-Pond), where he eventually wrote a series of reflective essays titled [Walden; or, Life in the Woods](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Walden). _[[Take our authors and poets quiz](https://www.britannica.com/quiz/the-literary-world-authors-poets).]_
